PS7 Q2(a): why are no cars traded?

Sellers thinkBuyers think
Suppose cars are being sold at some price P.
Seller
If cars sell for P, what does a seller get if they fix a car? And if they don’t?
Cars sell for P. If I fix a car, I get P − 1,200 − 300 = P − 1,500. If I don’t fix it, I get P − 1,200.
Seller
Can buyers tell whether a car was fixed? So what does the seller do?
Buyers can’t verify whether a car was fixed, so I get the same P either way.
P − 1,200 > P − 1,500, so I never fix.
Buyer
Buyers know how sellers think. What kind of car do they expect, and how much will they pay?
I know sellers never fix. So every car is a lemon, and I pay at most £1,000.
Seller
Buyers pay at most £1,000. Will sellers acquire cars that cost £1,200?
Buyers pay at most £1,000, but a car costs me £1,200 to acquire. So I don’t acquire any cars.
Equilibrium
So what is the equilibrium?
No cars are acquired or sold: expected value £1,000 < average cost £1,200.

PS7 Q2(b): sellers commit to repairing a share q

Buyers still cannot tell which car was fixed, but they know q (for example, it is required by law).

Sellers: average cost per car
AC(q) = 1,200 + 300q
Buyers: expected value of a car
EV(q) = q × 2,000 + (1 − q) × 1,000 = 1,000 + 1,000q
Why was there no trade in (a)?
There q = 0: EV = £1,000 < AC = £1,200. Buyers would pay less than a car costs.
So trade needs EV(q) ≥ AC(q)
1,000 + 1,000q ≥ 1,200 + 300q  ⇒  700q ≥ 200  ⇒  q ≥ 2/7 ≈ 28.6%
Below 2/7: no trade. At exactly 2/7: trade is possible, but with zero surplus. Above 2/7: trade with positive surplus.

Buyers are risk-neutral and pay their expected valuation.

Price
P = EV(q) = 1,000 + 1,000q
Seller’s profit per car
EV(q) − AC(q) = 700q − 200 (zero at q = 2/7)
Question
If sellers can commit to any q, which q do they choose?
Answer
q = 1: every car is fixed, and profit is £500 per car (= £2,000 − £1,500).
Fixing costs £300 and adds £1,000 of value, so this is also the efficient outcome.

A smart commitment (a warranty, certification, regulation) turns a market that does not exist into one that works. That is mechanism design, and it is why it is fun to be an economist.

Signalling and screening are responses to asymmetric information, where one party, such as a worker or a car seller, has private information about its type that the other party cannot observe. If types cannot be told apart, the uninformed side pays a single pooling wage or price, so high types are paid less than they are worth and low types more. This leads to adverse selection: low-quality types can crowd out high-quality ones, as in the market for lemons.

Signalling is undertaken by the agent with more information, to reveal it to the uninformed agent: for example, a car seller offers a warranty, or job applicants signal their ability through education and their CVs. In Spence’s model, education is a credible signal only if the wage gain covers the cost for high-ability workers but not for low-ability workers, so that only they acquire it, giving a separating equilibrium.

Screening is undertaken by the agent with less information, to discover the hidden information: for example, a firm offers a menu of contracts from which consumers self-select, employers sift through CVs, or a buyer pays a mechanic to check a used car.

Both can improve information in the market, but not necessarily efficiency. In the simple signalling model, education does not raise productivity, so total output is unchanged while education is costly: signalling worsened efficiency. Improved information need not improve gains to trade.

PS8 Q1(d): what must the courses achieve?

The firm wants to pay £1,000 to high types and £500 to low types, so it pays £1,000 only to workers with eH courses.

Goal 1: low types must not pretend to be high
When does a low type prefer to take no courses?

£500 > £1,000 − 200eH ⇒ eH > 2.5
